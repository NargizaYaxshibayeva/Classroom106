PART 4

Good afternoon. This is the third session of the series of lectures on wildlife. We're going to look at two studies on the habitats of birds in Australia.
The first study was carried out by R.B. Cunningham and his team in 1995. The primary goal was to detect the breeding habits of some birds, especially those protected species in urban regions. [Q31 → Protected]
One might expect that researchers had to count through binoculars. Instead, they managed to estimate with various standard distance sampling methods like random sampling. [Q32 → estimate]
In addition, a mapping system was used to study their companionship behavior. [Q33 → mapping]
Researchers attached identity tags to birds' legs transmitting radio signals, making it possible to assess the trends of birds over fifteen months. [Q34 → trends]
Many of the birds tagged had the tendency to nest in the same habitat where they grew up. This research also found that pollution had contributed greatly to the declining birth rates of some species. [Q35 → pollution]
A number of measures were taken by local animal conservation organizations to boost their alarming survival rates. [Q36 → survival] Breeding boxes were placed in trees, and protection guidelines were introduced.
A further measure was taken to ensure the safety of birds from wind farms. [Q37 → wind] A great deal of work was done on how to prevent them from colliding with turbines.

Then another piece of research was done by Conway in 2006. His team detected the calling frequency of seabirds flying at night. [Q38 → frequency] Acoustic signals are important communication tools for birds.
Surveyors also took recordings of the sounds of all the individual birds detected. [Q39 → Recordings] A tablet PC was used to process detailed information.
To minimize the disturbance, all the sounds were recorded from a distance using microphones to detect noise. [Q40 → distance]
They found the probability of call events was much higher for solitary birds. Increased calling activity in the presence of more profitable prey could be of crucial importance to seabirds that benefit from group foraging.
